How do I draw an MOT (maintenance of traffic) plan?

To draw an MOT plan, define the work and its traffic impact, pick the standard layout your agency accepts (often a typical application from MUTCD Part 6), then draw the four parts of the work zone with every sign, device and distance labeled. Then check it against your permit conditions and get it reviewed and approved before anyone sets a cone.

Note: "MOT" here means maintenance of traffic, the temporary traffic control around a work zone. It is not the vehicle "MOT test" used in some places.

A plain process

  1. Define the scope. Type of work, location, hours, duration, and which lanes, shoulders or sidewalks are closed. Note driveways, transit stops, schools and emergency access.
  2. Confirm the standard. MUTCD Part 6 covers temporary traffic control. Your road owner may add its own standard drawings, supplements and permit conditions. Use the latest edition they accept.
  3. Start from a typical application. Pick the layout that matches the work, such as a shoulder closure, lane closure or flagger operation. Treat it as a starting point, not a finished plan.
  4. Calculate distances. Use the posted speed and road geometry to set advance warning sign spacing, taper lengths, buffer space and device spacing from the tables in your standard.
  5. Draw the work zone. Show the advance warning area, transition area, activity area (buffer and work space) and termination area. Place signs, cones, drums, barricades and arrow boards. Hatch the work area.
  6. Plan for pedestrians and bikes. Show sidewalk closures, detours and accessible temporary routes.
  7. Add notes. Work hours, phases, contacts, flagger positions and any agency notes.
  8. Review and submit. A qualified reviewer checks it. Some agencies require an engineer's seal. Update the plan when conditions or phases change.

Common follow-up questions

Is an MOT plan the same as a traffic control plan (TCP)? In practice, yes. Both describe how traffic, pedestrians and workers move around a temporary work zone.

Do I need an engineer to stamp it? It depends on the agency. Some require a licensed engineer's seal; confirm before you submit.

Can I draw it by hand or in a PDF editor? Yes, if the agency accepts it. Software helps with consistent symbols, scale and clean exports.

What should every MOT plan show? Work limits, closed lanes, taper and buffer lengths, all signs and devices with spacing, pedestrian routes, phases and notes.
